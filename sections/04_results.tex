\section{Results and Discussion}

The performance of MBA-RL was evaluated using cell-specific models identified from three Samsung 40T 21700 cells, denoted as Cell 1, Cell 2, and Cell 3. Each cell has a nominal capacity of 4000 mAh and a maximum discharge current of 35 A. Voltage and temperature measurements were collected using the NEWARE CT-4008-5V20A-A platform shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:experiment_Schematic}. The identified SPMe models were then used for policy training and evaluation with independently controlled charging currents.

\subsection{Performance Evaluation of MBA-RL}

The identified parameters were incorporated into the PyBaMM models \cite{29}, which were used for policy training and evaluation. The policy weights were fixed during testing. A task was considered complete when every cell reached the reference SOC and the SOC standard deviation remained below the balancing threshold for 270 s without violating the operating limits. The two initial SOC vectors, $Set\;A=[10,20,30]^{\mathrm{T}}$ and $Set\;B=[30,50,70]^{\mathrm{T}}$, are used as representative examples in Fig.~\ref{fig:overall_figure} and Table~\ref{tab:soc_vectors}.

During execution, each MBA-RL agent selects the charging current of its cell from its local observation and action-observation history. In the comparison, DQL additionally controls a cell-to-cell energy-transfer topology, while the CC-CV baseline activates the equalization topology during the CV phase.

Fig.~\ref{fig:overall_figure} compares the charging trajectories of the three methods. In both representative cases, MBA-RL and DQL first satisfy $\sigma\leq\beta$ earlier than CC-CV. However, DQL and CC-CV reach this condition before all cells satisfy the terminal SOC requirement. In contrast, MBA-RL first reaches the balancing condition after all cells have exceeded the reference SOC, as shown in Table~\ref{tab:soc_vectors}. Therefore, the first time at which $\sigma\leq\beta$ alone does not represent completion of the charging task. The following comparisons consider task completion, time score, accumulated SOC imbalance $\int\sigma(t)\,\mathrm{d}t$, and constraint satisfaction.

\subsubsection{Effect of the Safety Mechanism}

We first isolate the effect of the safety mechanism during execution by applying it to a fixed policy. The number of tests satisfying the operating constraints increases from 3/52 to 52/52, while task completion increases from 1/52 to 35/52. When the same mechanism is also used during training, all 52 tests reach the charging objective, and the accumulated SOC imbalance $\int\sigma(t)\,\mathrm{d}t$ decreases from 469.97 to 197.34 SOC fraction s. These results show that the safety mechanism prevents unsafe actions during execution, while its inclusion during training also improves task completion and SOC balancing.

\subsubsection{Comparative Charging Performance}

The learned methods were evaluated over 60 cases, consisting of 12 unseen initial SOC conditions for each of five independent training runs. A failed task is assigned a time score of 5670 s, and $\pm$ denotes the standard deviation across the five training-run means. All methods complete 60/60 evaluations except continuous MAPPO, which completes 59/60.

Among the value-based methods using the same discrete current set, MBA-RL reduces accumulated SOC imbalance by 12.65\% relative to shared IQL and by 10.82\% relative to centralized DQN. Discrete MAPPO and SAC obtain lower numerical scores in Table~\ref{tab:modern_baselines}. Overall, MBA-RL achieves competitive charging performance while combining independently controlled cell currents, SOC balancing without a dedicated equalization topology, and the model-based safety mechanism in a single framework.

Fig.~\ref{fig:discrete_trajectories} further compares MBA-RL with shared IQL and centralized DQN under the same discrete current set. In this representative case, MBA-RL reaches the target region earlier than shared IQL and ends with a lower SOC standard deviation than centralized DQN. The results over all unseen initial SOC conditions are summarized in Table~\ref{tab:modern_baselines}.

\subsubsection{Ablation and Sensitivity Analysis}

We evaluate the network components and reward design through the ablations in Fig.~\ref{fig:component_ablation}, followed by tests of operating variations and battery-pack size.

\begin{figure}[!t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.95\columnwidth]{component_ablation.pdf}
\caption{Key component ablations over 12 unseen conditions and five independent training runs. Open circles show each run's accumulated SOC imbalance; diamonds show the five-run means. The dashed line marks the MBA-RL mean, and the right-hand labels give completed evaluations.}
\label{fig:component_ablation}
\end{figure}

Removing either parameter sharing or the balancing reward causes one of the five training runs to fail across all 12 test conditions, reducing task completion from 60/60 to 48/60. The mean accumulated SOC imbalance increases by 80.47\% without parameter sharing and by 59.07\% without the balancing reward. Removing the mixing network retains 60/60 completion but increases the accumulated imbalance by 14.48\%. These results show that parameter sharing and the balancing reward are important for consistent performance, while the mixing network further improves SOC balancing.

The frozen policy completes all 100 tests covering the specified $\pm10\%$ parameter variations, fixed sensor bias, a 15~$^\circ$C ambient condition, and a 30\% reduction in available current. Task completion decreases in the presence of observation noise, a one-decision delay, and ambient temperatures of 34--35~$^\circ$C. The policy is therefore more sensitive to observation errors, delay, and high ambient temperature than to the tested model and actuation variations.

The same MBA-RL architecture was trained separately for battery packs with 3, 6, and 12 independently controlled cells. The median training time increases from 1.715 to 6.450 h as the pack size increases, while task completion decreases from 59/60 for the three-cell setting to 29/36 for the 12-cell setting. The 12-cell evaluation contains 12 conditions over three independent training runs, whereas the three-cell evaluation uses five runs because of the higher simulation and training cost at the larger scale.

Policy inference remains approximately 2--3 ms, while the safety processing time increases from 1.706 to 7.549 s. Both remain below the 90 s decision interval. The computational cost is therefore acceptable at the tested scales, although task completion decreases as the number of cells increases. The larger simulated packs are constructed by repeating the three identified cell models and do not include series-circuit, shared-power, or thermal coupling.
