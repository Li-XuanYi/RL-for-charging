\section{Introduction}

\IEEEPARstart{L}{ithium-ion} batteries have been widely adopted in electric vehicles (EVs) because of their high energy density, low self-discharge rate, long cycle life, and environmental advantages \cite{1, 2}. However, a single lithium-ion cell cannot satisfy the high voltage and capacity requirements of practical EV applications \cite{3, 4}. Therefore, battery systems typically consist of hundreds or even thousands of individual cells. Manufacturing inconsistencies and variations in operating conditions inevitably lead to differences in internal states and aging characteristics among cells, which can reduce the usable capacity, charging efficiency, and durability of the battery pack while increasing safety risks \cite{5, 6}. Consequently, an effective charging equalization strategy is required to reduce charging time while maintaining inter-cell balance under operating and safety constraints.

Most existing charging equalization strategies combine an equalization topology with a balancing algorithm. The equalization topology provides energy-transfer or dissipation paths among cells, while the balancing algorithm determines how these paths are operated to redistribute charge and energy within the battery pack \cite{7, 8}. Different combinations of topology and control strategy can result in substantially different balancing performance. Therefore, the design of a practical equalization strategy generally requires a trade-off between balancing performance, energy efficiency, system complexity, and implementation cost \cite{ma2024two}.

\noindent\textit{Equalization topologies:}
Equalization topologies are generally categorized as active or passive. Active equalization transfers excess energy from high-energy cells to low-energy cells through switched capacitors, inductors \cite{9}, transformers \cite{10}, or DC-DC converters \cite{11}. Although active balancing can improve energy utilization, the corresponding circuitry becomes increasingly complicated as the number of cells and series-parallel connections increases, resulting in higher system complexity and implementation cost. Passive equalization, in contrast, typically connects discharge resistors in parallel with individual cells \cite{12}. Excess energy from high-energy cells is dissipated as heat through these resistors. Although passive balancing is relatively simple to implement, the associated energy dissipation reduces overall efficiency. Thus, both conventional active and passive equalization require additional energy-transfer or dissipation paths, introducing either conversion losses or additional topology complexity.

\noindent\textit{Balancing algorithms:}
Traditional balancing algorithms are often built upon constant-current constant-voltage (CC-CV) charging to achieve voltage or SOC equalization among battery cells. Aizpuru {\it et al.} \cite{13} combined CC-CV charging with single-cell experimental data to construct a passive balancing system. Hoque {\it et al.} \cite{14} employed particle swarm optimization (PSO) to optimize a CC-CV balancing controller. Although CC-CV-based methods are straightforward to implement, they generally provide limited adaptability to changes in battery states and operating conditions and often rely on conservative operating constraints.

With the development of advanced control methods, model-based and intelligent control strategies have increasingly been introduced into battery balancing. In \cite{15}, fuzzy logic control was employed to adaptively regulate the equalization process. Fan {\it et al.} \cite{16} proposed a fast active-balancing strategy based on model predictive control. These approaches improve the flexibility and control accuracy of battery equalization, but their ability to accommodate changing battery characteristics during charging remains limited.

Deep reinforcement learning (DRL) provides an alternative approach by learning charging policies through interactions with the battery environment. Yang {\it et al.} \cite{17} proposed a balancing-aware fast-charging control framework based on DRL, while \cite{18} introduced a DRL-based controller for active equalization of redundant battery systems. By interacting with dynamic environments, these approaches can learn adaptive charging policies without relying entirely on manually designed control rules. Nevertheless, existing DRL-based balancing methods still exhibit two important limitations. First, they typically rely on an additional equalization topology to redistribute charge or energy among cells, which introduces extra energy-transfer paths and implementation complexity. Second, balancing is often treated as an intermediate charging objective, while the possibility that SOC imbalance may re-emerge during subsequent charging has received less attention.

Multi-agent reinforcement learning (MARL) has been widely applied to cooperative control and decision-making problems \cite{19, 20}. Liang {\it et al.} \cite{21} employed MARL for real-time management of a battery swapping and charging system, while Dong {\it et al.} \cite{22} applied MARL to peak-shaving control. Charging balancing control naturally exhibits a cooperative multi-agent structure: each cell has its own local electrochemical and thermal states and can be assigned an independently regulated charging current, while all cells share the pack-level objectives of fast charging and SOC balancing. This structure motivates a cooperative MARL formulation in which cell-level charging decisions are coordinated directly, without relying on a dedicated equalization topology.

Motivated by this cooperative structure, this paper proposes a multi-balancing-agent reinforcement learning (MBA-RL) framework for lithium-ion battery charging control. In MBA-RL, each agent regulates the charging current of one cell, while a shared pack-level objective coordinates charging progress and SOC balancing across the battery pack. The framework therefore realizes balancing through coordinated cell-level charging rather than through a separate energy-transfer topology. In addition, a model-based safety-constrained execution mechanism is incorporated to screen the requested charging currents before they are applied to the battery models. Cell-specific electrochemical-thermal models identified from batteries with different cycling histories are further used to evaluate the framework under cell-to-cell variability and unseen operating conditions.

The main contributions of this paper are summarized as follows:

\begin{enumerate}

    \item An MBA-RL charging control framework is developed to coordinate independently regulated cell-level charging currents without relying on a dedicated equalization topology. Through cooperative MARL, local charging decisions are coordinated toward fast charging and pack-level SOC balancing.

    \item A model-based safety-constrained execution mechanism is integrated into the MARL framework. Requested charging currents are screened before execution according to predicted voltage, temperature, and SOC constraints, enabling the learned charging policy to operate under the prescribed cell-level safety limits.

    \item Comprehensive evaluations are conducted using cell-specific electrochemical-thermal models identified from cells with different cycling histories. The experiments examine unseen initial SOC conditions, operating variations, component ablations, and different pack sizes to evaluate the effectiveness, robustness, and limitations of the proposed framework.

\end{enumerate}
